Section 1

The format on paper

Two conservatives across from two liberals at one table in the middle of Tee & Tap, with Mark in the moderator's chair. A claim goes up, each side takes a position, and the other side pushes back. Mark keeps it fair, keeps it moving, and has the only hand that can stop it. No script, no pre-cleared talking points, and the camera does not stop.

A taping is about three hours door to door: 45 minutes of check-in and paperwork verification, a 15-minute seat-and-sound check, roughly 90 minutes of recording across four or five prompts, and a short wrap. That yields one 60-minute episode plus 8 to 12 clips.

Cadence. Start with one taping every other week so the bench can catch up with the schedule, then move to weekly once each side has at least eight pre-screened people ready to sit. Pick the night by the bar's calendar; nothing in this plan depends on which night of the week it is.

What a good table looks like

Within each side of two, cast for two different jobs rather than two copies of one person:

  • The anchor is calm and articulate, the person the other side has to take seriously. Usually the oldest at the table.
  • The heat is passionate and quick, willing to interrupt, and the reason the clips travel.
  • The story is the swap: when the night's prompts fit, replace the heat with someone who has standing from their own life, a nurse on healthcare, a veteran on foreign policy, a landlord or a tenant on housing.

Mix ages, neighborhoods, and backgrounds inside each side. Cast both sides before confirming either, so a strong red bench does not pull a weak blue one onto camera.

Site, ads, and packet now say the same thing. Two-versus-two with Mark moderating, $35 an hour and about $105 for the recording, food and drinks included, show up sober. Any future change to format, pay, or the alcohol rule goes to all three at once.

Every taping books six people: four seats and one alternate per side. The alternates come to the bar, eat, and get a small show-up fee whether or not they sit. Section 4

Screening and vetting

The screening call is where the show is actually cast. It is also the only line of defense against the two things that can sink an episode: someone who cannot hold their composure, and someone who is there to hurt a specific person.

  1. Application landsThe form captures side, contact, pitch, availability, socials, and an optional video. Read the pitch the same day. Anyone who cannot write three sentences about what they believe is a pass.
  2. Screening invite within 48 hoursSend the scheduling link and the housekeeping paragraph: filmed and published, standard release, show up sober, pay. Anyone who bounces off that paragraph saved everyone a call.
  3. The 15-minute video callSix questions, scored 1 to 5 on conviction, composure, clarity, camera, and fit. The script and the red flags are in the kit. Cast at 18 or more of 25, bench at 15 to 17.
  4. The ten-minute searchGoogle the name plus Pittsburgh. Look at the public socials they gave you. You are looking for threats, harassment, doxxing, a current candidacy or party job they did not disclose, or a stated plan to "expose" someone. Note what you found on the submission in the admin dashboard.
  5. Cast the table, both sides at onceAnchor and heat on each side, one alternate per side, ages and backgrounds mixed. Mark signs off on the six names.
  6. Confirmation email with the e-sign envelopeDocuments 01 through 05 in one envelope, due 48 hours before call time. Unsigned at the deadline means the alternate is promoted and a new alternate is called from the bench.
  7. Day-before textCall time, address, photo ID, wardrobe, no drinking. Reply YES to confirm. No reply by noon on taping day means the alternate is warned to be ready.
Section 5

Paperwork and safety

The legal packet in the folder has nine documents. Panelists sign five of them; the rest are for the venue, the door, and the floor manager. The packet is drafted for Pennsylvania, which is an all-party-consent state for recording and enforces liability waivers only when they are clear and conspicuous. A Pennsylvania attorney should review it before the first signature.

Three layers of consent

Written, at the door, and on camera. The e-signature is the contract. The floor manager confirms it at check-in against a photo ID and reads the eight-line conduct summary. Then Mark reads the consent script from the Day-of Sheet before the first prompt and each panelist says their name and "I consent" on the recording, and the timecode goes on the sheet. If anyone declines at that moment, the alternate sits and the script runs again.

Show up sober, drinks at the table

Panelists arrive sober and are checked at the door. Once recording starts, the bar may serve them at the table under its normal responsible-service rules, and Mark or the floor manager can cut anyone off or pull them from the set. This is the packet's biggest liability question: people drinking on camera during a political argument is exactly what the waiver is trying to cover. The attorney should read the alcohol sections of the waiver, the code of conduct, and the location release with that in mind. Bar staff are briefed not to serve panelist lanyards before recording starts.

Insurance to arrange before the first taping

  • Media liability covering defamation, privacy, and intellectual property claims from what panelists say on air. Most small producers skip it; a political debate show should not.
  • General liability for the production, with Tee & Tap named as additional insured on taping days. Check whether the bar's own policy excludes filming.
  • Equipment coverage for the cameras and audio gear on location.

Two entities, two signatures. Even if the same people own Open Pod Talk and Tee & Tap, sign the Location Release. It keeps the bar's liquor liability and the show's media liability from bleeding into each other, and it is the first document a broker will ask for.

Section 9

What we measure

One row per week in a shared sheet, filled in at the Wednesday post-mortem. Five minutes, and it tells you which side is thin and which channel is paying for itself.

  • Applications by side, and the ratio between them. Under 40 percent on either side means that side's ad and org outreach need more spend this week.
  • Screening booked rate (calls held ÷ applications) and pass rate (18-plus scores ÷ calls held).
  • Cast-to-show rate, and the count of alternates who ended up sitting.
  • Bench depth by side: passed, unseated, still available. The weekly-cadence trigger is eight per side.
  • Cost per application for boosted posts (spend ÷ applications with a Facebook source) and for Craigslist (fee ÷ applications).
  • Incidents, by count and type, from the Day-of Sheet log. The target is zero and the trend matters more than the number.

Section 11

Risks and how we handle them

RiskWhat we do about it
No-shows leave a side shortOne alternate per side at every taping, paid a show-up fee. Day-before text with a required YES. Stipend paid after wrap, never before.
Lopsided applications, usually a thin conservative benchTrack sides separately. Red-only ad creative. Organization outreach weighted to whichever side is thin. Never confirm one side before the other is cast.
Plants, trolls, and "exposers"Screening call plus the ten-minute search. Anyone who wants to humiliate a named person, appear masked, or will not say what they believe in their own words does not sit.
Defamation of a real person on airPanelist warranties and indemnity in the release. Mark can cut and move on. Producer controls the edit. Media liability insurance. Publish full episodes alongside clips so no one can claim a clip fabricated the context.
Alcohol incidentArrive-sober check at the door; no service before recording starts; after that the bar serves at its discretion and Mark or the floor manager can cut anyone off or pull them from the set. Safe-ride plan captured on the information form. Attorney reviews the alcohol clauses specifically.
Harassment or doxxing of a panelist after releaseFirst-name-and-initial option on screen. Comment moderation on release day. A clear reporting address in the code of conduct and a promise to act on reports.
Meta rejects the boosted postComplete social-issues authorization in Week 0. Run the disclaimer. Appeal once as a casting notice for a program, then run the authorized version. Groups and the Event carry the load meanwhile.
Craigslist flags the listingOne post, one category, renewed rather than reposted, two rotating versions. If ghosted, wait 48 hours and post the other version from the same account.
A panelist wants out after tapingThe release is irrevocable and says so. Be kind, offer the first-name-only graphic if that helps, and hold the line; the release exists for exactly this.
The packet is used before a lawyer sees itDo not. The ads promise a "standard release"; the attorney review is what makes it one. Week 0 exists for this.
